How Electricians Can Handle Extra Work and Change Orders

TheFixer Designs guide · November 8, 2026 · 5 min read

Running a small electrical business is hard work, and it is easy for scope creep to sneak in. A customer might point to a new outlet and say, 'While you are here, can you just add this one?' It feels harmless, but these small requests add up. They eat into your time, drain your materials budget, and can lead to unhappy clients if the final bill is higher than expected. The key to handling extra work is not saying no, but managing the process clearly. This guide shows you how to keep your projects on track and your profits healthy by handling change orders with confidence and clarity.

On this page

  1. Pause and Document Immediately
  2. Calculate the True Cost
  3. Get Written Approval Before Starting
  4. Show Changes Clearly on the Invoice

Pause and Document Immediately

The moment a client asks for something outside the original plan, stop what you are doing. Do not start the work immediately. Even if you think it will only take ten minutes, that time costs money. Your first step is to acknowledge the request politely but firmly state that you need to document it first.

Write down exactly what the client is asking for. Be specific. Instead of writing 'add light,' write 'install one 6-inch recessed LED downlight in the master bedroom closet.' This clarity prevents misunderstandings later. If the client is not present, take a photo of the area and note the request in your job file or app right away.

  • Stop work before starting the new task.
  • Write down the exact scope of the new request.
  • Take photos of the location if the client is not there.

Calculate the True Cost

Pricing extra work is not just about the wire and the fixture. You must account for your time, travel, and overhead. Start by estimating how long the new task will take. Use your standard hourly rate for the labor portion. Do not guess; use your historical data on how long similar tasks take you.

Next, add the cost of materials. If you buy the part specifically for this job, use the actual cost. If you have it in stock, use your wholesale cost plus a small markup to cover storage and handling. Finally, add a portion of your overhead. This covers your truck, insurance, tools, and admin time. This ensures you are not subsidizing the client's extra requests with your own profit.

Present this as a clear breakdown. Show the labor hours, the material cost, and the overhead fee separately. This transparency helps the client understand why the price is what it is. It shows them that you are running a professional business, not just guessing numbers.

  • Estimate labor hours based on your standard rate.
  • Add material costs, including any markup for stock items.
  • Include a portion of overhead for tools and admin.

Get Written Approval Before Starting

Never start extra work without explicit approval. A verbal 'yes' is not enough. In the heat of the moment, clients may agree to a price but later dispute it when they see the invoice. Protect yourself by getting everything in writing. This can be an email, a text message, or a signed change order form.

Send the quote for the extra work to the client immediately. Clearly state the total price and what it includes. Ask them to reply with 'Approved' or sign the document. Once you have that confirmation, you have the green light to proceed. This step removes ambiguity and ensures both parties are on the same page before a single wire is stripped.

If the client hesitates or asks for a lower price, discuss it now. You can offer to adjust the scope to fit their budget, but do not just lower your price without adjusting the work. This protects your value and keeps the relationship honest.

  • Require written confirmation via email or text.
  • Send a clear quote with the total price.
  • Wait for approval before touching any tools.

Show Changes Clearly on the Invoice

When the job is done, your invoice must reflect the original agreement and the new work separately. Do not lump everything into one big number. List the original scope of work first, with its agreed-upon price. Then, add a separate line item for the change order.

Label this line item clearly, such as 'Change Order #1: Additional Recessed Light.' Include the details you documented earlier: the labor hours, the material cost, and the overhead fee. This makes it easy for the client to see exactly what they are paying for. It also makes it easier for you to track your profitability on each specific task.

If there are multiple change orders, list them sequentially. This creates a clear paper trail. If the client has questions, you can point to the specific line item and the corresponding approval email. This professionalism builds trust and reduces the chance of payment delays.

  • List original work and extra work on separate lines.
  • Label change orders clearly with details.
  • Maintain a clear paper trail for all adjustments.

Handling extra work is a normal part of being an electrician. By pausing to document, pricing accurately, getting written approval, and invoicing clearly, you protect your time and your reputation. These steps turn potential conflicts into opportunities to show your professionalism. When clients see that you are organized and fair, they are more likely to trust you with future projects.

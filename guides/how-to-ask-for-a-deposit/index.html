How to Ask for a Deposit (and Show It on Your Invoice)

TheFixer Designs guide · 2026-10-31

A deposit protects your time, covers materials you buy up front, and shows that a client is serious about the booking. Yet many small service businesses skip it because asking feels awkward, or they take one and then show it so unclearly that the final invoice causes confusion. This guide covers when a deposit makes sense, how to ask for it in plain language, and how to record it on your estimate and invoice so the balance due is obvious to everyone.

When a deposit makes sense

Not every job needs a deposit. A short call-out paid on the day is usually fine without one. A deposit is worth asking for when you commit something before the work starts.

  • You are buying materials or parts specifically for this client.
  • You are holding a date that you could otherwise sell to someone else, such as an event or a large booking.
  • The job runs over several days or weeks.
  • The client is new and the job is larger than your usual work.
  • Your schedule is full and last-minute cancellations would leave a gap.

How to ask without awkwardness

The easiest way to ask is to make the deposit part of your normal process rather than a special request. Put it in your estimate, mention it when you send the estimate, and explain what it covers.

Sample wording: "To confirm the booking, I take a deposit when the estimate is approved. It covers the materials I order for your job and holds your date in my calendar. The balance is due when the work is finished."

Clients rarely object when the deposit is presented as standard practice with a clear reason. If someone pushes back hard on a reasonable deposit for a large job, that is useful information before you commit your time.

Show the deposit on the estimate

Your estimate should show the full price of the job first, then the deposit as a separate line, then the remaining balance. That way the client sees the total commitment and exactly what they pay now.

State when the deposit is due, how to pay it, and what happens if the job is cancelled. Keep this short and specific. A client who understands the terms at the start is far less likely to dispute them later.

Record it correctly on the final invoice

This is where most confusion happens. The final invoice should never look like the client owes the full price again. List the work as normal so the invoice shows the full job total, then add a clear line for the deposit already paid, with the date it was received, and finish with the balance due.

If the job changed after the estimate, show the approved changes as their own lines before the deposit is subtracted. The client can then follow the numbers from the estimate, through the changes, to what they owe now.

  • Job total, including any approved changes.
  • Less deposit received, with the date.
  • Balance due, with the due date and how to pay.

Cancellations and refunds

Decide your cancellation terms before you take your first deposit, and write them on the estimate. Common approaches include keeping the part of the deposit already spent on materials, or keeping the deposit if a booked date is cancelled at short notice. Whatever you choose, apply it consistently and explain it plainly.

Rules about deposits, refunds and consumer rights vary by location. If you are unsure what you are allowed to keep, check the guidance where you work before you set your terms.

How to Price a House Cleaning Job

TheFixer Designs guide · 2026-10-31

Pricing a house cleaning job by guessing from the doorway is how cleaners end up working two hours for the price of one. A short, repeatable pricing routine fixes that. You look at the home with a clear checklist, turn what you see into time, add your supplies and travel, and send a written estimate the client can say yes to. This guide walks through that routine step by step so every quote you send is consistent, fair to the client and worth your time.

Start with a walkthrough, not a phone guess

Whenever you can, see the home before you quote. A short walkthrough in person or a video call shows you what a phone description never will: how cluttered the surfaces are, whether there are pets, how many bathrooms really need deep work, and whether the client expects a maintenance clean or a first-time reset.

Use the same walkthrough questions every time so your quotes stay comparable from one home to the next.

  • How many bedrooms, bathrooms and living areas will you clean?
  • Is this a first clean, a regular visit, or a move-in or move-out clean?
  • Are there pets, heavy buildup, or rooms the client wants skipped?
  • Which supplies does the client want you to bring, and are there any products to avoid?
  • Where will you park, and how easy is it to get in and out?

Turn the home into time

Clients pay for results, but your costs are driven by time. After the walkthrough, estimate how long each area will take you at a steady, careful pace. Bathrooms and kitchens usually take the longest, and first cleans take longer than repeat visits because buildup has to be removed once before regular maintenance becomes quick.

Keep a simple log of how long real jobs take compared with your estimate. After a few weeks you will have your own reference times for a bedroom, a bathroom and a kitchen, which makes every future quote faster and more accurate.

Add supplies, travel and the extras that eat your margin

Time is only part of the cost. Before you set a price, add the things that quietly reduce what you take home.

  • Cleaning products, cloths, vacuum bags and replacement tools used on the job
  • Travel time and fuel to reach the home and get back
  • Parking costs or extra time spent carrying equipment
  • Time spent on scheduling, messages and invoicing for that client

Price the add-ons separately

Inside the oven, inside the fridge, interior windows, baseboards, laundry and organizing all take real extra time. Listing them as separate add-ons keeps your base price clean and easy to compare, and it lets clients choose exactly what they want instead of paying for a bundle they do not need.

Separate add-ons also protect you on the day. If a client asks for something extra while you are there, you can point to the add-on list and agree on it on the spot instead of absorbing the work for free.

Choose hourly, flat or package pricing

Hourly pricing is simple and safe for first cleans where the condition is uncertain. Flat pricing per visit is easier for clients to understand and works well once you know the home. Recurring packages, such as weekly or every other week, reward regular clients and give you predictable income.

Many cleaners use all three: hourly for a first deep clean, then a flat price for the recurring visits that follow. Whatever you choose, work out your hourly target first and check that every flat or package price still meets it.

Put the estimate in writing

A written estimate prevents most pricing disagreements before they start. List the rooms included, the type of clean, every add-on, what supplies you bring, how often you will visit, and how and when you expect payment. Note anything that would change the price, such as heavier buildup than expected or extra rooms added on the day.

When the job is done, turn the approved estimate into an invoice with the same line items so the client sees exactly what they agreed to. Check local rules for anything specific to your area, such as sales tax on services.

Good pricing is a routine, not a feeling. Walk through the home, convert what you see into time, add supplies and travel, list extras separately, and send a clear written estimate. Do that for every job and your quotes become faster to write, easier for clients to accept, and far more likely to pay you properly for the work.

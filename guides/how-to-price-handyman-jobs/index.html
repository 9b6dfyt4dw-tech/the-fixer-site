How to Price Handyman Jobs: Hourly, Flat Rate or Per Task

TheFixer Designs guide · 2026-10-31

Handyman work is hard to price because no two jobs are the same. One visit might be a single shelf, the next a list of ten small repairs across a house. Charge too little and long days barely cover your costs. Quote too vaguely and clients get surprised by the final invoice. This guide explains the three common pricing methods, when each one fits, and how to account for the costs that are easy to forget, so your estimates are fair, consistent and easy for clients to approve.

Start with what a working hour costs you

Whatever pricing method you use, you need to know your own costs first. A working hour is not only the time you spend on the job. It also carries travel, quoting, buying materials, admin, tools, insurance, vehicle costs and the hours you spend not getting paid at all.

Add up your yearly business costs and the income you need, then divide by the hours you can realistically bill in a year, not the hours you are busy. That gives you a floor. Any price below it means you are paying to do the work. Local demand, your experience and your specialties decide how far above that floor you can go.

Hourly pricing

Hourly pricing works well when the scope is unclear, such as tracing a problem, working in an older home, or helping a client who keeps adding small tasks as you go. It protects you from jobs that grow.

The downside is that clients cannot easily predict the total, and some feel they are paying for slow work. If you charge hourly, give a time estimate range up front and explain what could push it higher. Many handymen set a minimum charge, for example one hour, so short visits still cover travel and setup.

Flat-rate and per-task pricing

Flat-rate pricing means one agreed price for a defined job. Per-task pricing is a menu of common jobs, such as mounting a TV, hanging a door or assembling furniture, each with its own price. Clients like both because they know the total before you arrive.

These methods reward you for being efficient, since a job you finish quickly still earns the agreed price. The risk is a job that turns out bigger than expected. Protect yourself by describing exactly what the price includes and what counts as extra.

  • Define the task: what you will do, and what is not included.
  • State any assumptions, such as standard wall type or existing fixings.
  • Explain how surprises are handled, such as a quick approval before extra work.
  • Review your task prices from time to time as costs change.

Materials, travel and small-job bundles

Decide how you handle materials before you quote. Some handymen pass parts through at cost plus a handling charge for the time spent sourcing them. Others ask the client to buy specific items. Either works, as long as the estimate says which one applies.

Travel is easy to forget on small jobs. A trip charge or a minimum visit price keeps short jobs worthwhile. Bundling also helps both sides: encourage clients to save up several small repairs for one visit, and price the visit as a block of time or a list of tasks.

Put it in a clear estimate

Whichever method you choose, write it down. A good handyman estimate lists each task, the pricing method for each, materials, any trip or minimum charge, and the payment terms. It also notes how long the estimate is valid and how changes will be approved.

When the work is done, turn the approved estimate into the invoice with the same line items, adding only changes the client agreed to. Matching numbers make clients comfortable paying straight away.

There is no single right way to price handyman work. Know your true hourly cost, use hourly pricing for open-ended jobs and flat or per-task prices for well-defined ones, and always account for materials and travel. Then put every detail in a written estimate so the final invoice holds no surprises.

How to Ask a Client for a Late Payment (Polite Reminder Wording)

TheFixer Designs guide · October 31, 2026 · 4 min read

Almost every small service business eventually sends an invoice that goes past its due date. It rarely means the client refuses to pay. More often the invoice got buried, went to the wrong person, or the client is waiting on something they never told you about. A clear, polite reminder sequence gets most of those invoices paid without damaging the relationship. This guide walks through what to check before you send anything, sample wording for each stage, and the small changes to your invoices that make late payments less likely in the first place.

On this page

  2. The friendly first reminder
  3. A firmer follow-up
  4. The final notice
  5. Make the next invoice harder to ignore

Check your own invoice first

Before you chase anyone, make sure the problem is not on your side. A reminder that points to an invoice with the wrong amount or a missing payment link is awkward to walk back.

Open the original invoice and confirm the basics. It also helps to search your inbox for any reply from the client you might have missed, such as a question about a line item.

  • The invoice number, date and due date are shown clearly.
  • The client name and billing email are correct.
  • The total matches the agreed estimate or any approved changes.
  • Payment instructions are on the invoice: how to pay, and who to pay.
  • You actually sent it, and it did not bounce.

The friendly first reminder

Send the first reminder a day or two after the due date. Keep it short and assume good intent. Attach the invoice again so the client does not have to search for it.

Sample wording: "Hi Jordan, a quick note that invoice 1042 for the kitchen repair was due on Friday. I have attached a copy in case it got lost. You can pay by bank transfer or card using the details on the invoice. Let me know if you have any questions. Thanks again for the work."

Notice what this message does. It names the invoice, the job and the due date, makes paying easy, and invites a question instead of an argument.

A firmer follow-up

If a week passes with no reply, send a second message. Stay polite, but be more direct and ask for a specific response.

Sample wording: "Hi Jordan, I am following up on invoice 1042, which is now one week overdue. Could you let me know when I can expect payment? If something about the invoice needs correcting, I am happy to sort it out today."

If you have a phone number, a short call at this stage often works better than another email. Many overdue invoices are resolved in one friendly conversation.

The final notice

When an invoice is several weeks overdue and the client has gone quiet, send a final notice in writing. State the facts, the amount, and what happens next. Only mention a late fee or a next step if it was in your written terms or agreement, and only if you are prepared to follow through.

Sample wording: "Hi Jordan, invoice 1042 for the kitchen repair remains unpaid and is now four weeks overdue. Please arrange payment by the end of next week. If I do not hear from you by then, I will need to pause any further bookings and follow up through other channels as set out in our agreement."

Keep a record of every reminder you send, with dates. If you ever need outside help, a clear paper trail makes everything simpler. For anything involving legal action or collections, check the rules where you work or speak to a qualified advisor.

Make the next invoice harder to ignore

Most late payments are prevented before the job starts, not after it ends. A few habits make a real difference.

  • Agree payment terms in writing before you start, and repeat them on the invoice.
  • Take a deposit on larger jobs so the balance is smaller and easier to pay.
  • Send the invoice the same day the job is finished, while the work is fresh.
  • Offer at least two simple ways to pay.
  • Use a clear due date, such as a specific calendar date, instead of vague wording.

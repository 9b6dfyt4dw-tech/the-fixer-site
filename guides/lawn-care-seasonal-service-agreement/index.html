How to Write a Simple Seasonal Lawn Care Agreement

TheFixer Designs guide · November 9, 2026 · 3 min read

Seasonal lawn care agreements are the backbone of a steady small business. They set clear expectations for both you and your client, reducing misunderstandings and ensuring you get paid on time. This guide walks you through the essential elements of a simple, practical contract that protects your time and your reputation.

On this page

  1. Define the Scope of Work
  2. Set Visit Frequency and Season Dates
  3. Handle Weather and Skipped Visits
  4. Billing Schedule and Payment Terms
  5. Termination and Cancellation

Define the Scope of Work

Start by clearly describing what you will do. Avoid vague terms like 'maintain the lawn.' Instead, list specific tasks such as mowing, edging, blowing debris, and trimming hedges. Specify which areas are included, such as the front yard, back yard, or side strips, and explicitly state what is excluded, like garden beds or high-maintenance shrubs.

This clarity prevents scope creep, where clients expect extra work that was not part of the deal. If you offer add-on services like fertilizing or aeration, list them separately so the client knows exactly what they are paying for in the base agreement.

Set Visit Frequency and Season Dates

Specify the exact days of the week you will visit, such as 'every Tuesday' or 'the first and third Saturday of the month.' Consistency helps you plan your route and helps the client know when to expect you. Also, define the start and end dates of the season. For example, the agreement might run from April 1st to October 31st, or it could be tied to the first and last frost dates in your specific region.

Handle Weather and Skipped Visits

Weather is the biggest variable in lawn care. You need a clear policy for when visits are skipped due to rain, extreme heat, or frozen ground. State that if the ground is too wet to mow without damaging the turf, the visit will be rescheduled. Decide if you will automatically reschedule to the next day or if the client must request a new date.

Be transparent about how many free reschedules are included. If a client cancels with less than 24 hours' notice, you may charge a fee or deduct from their account. This protects your income if you have already traveled to the site or blocked out your schedule.

Billing Schedule and Payment Terms

Decide how you will bill: monthly in advance, monthly in arrears, or as a lump sum for the season. Monthly in advance is often best for cash flow, as it ensures you are paid before you do the work. Clearly state the due date and the accepted payment methods, such as bank transfer, check, or digital payment apps.

Explain how you will calculate the price. Start by estimating the time it takes to complete the job, including travel time. Add your cost of fuel, equipment maintenance, and labor. Then, add a margin for profit. This ensures your rate covers all costs and leaves room for growth. Do not guess; use your actual time logs to determine a fair rate.

Termination and Cancellation

Include a clause that allows either party to end the agreement. Typically, this requires a written notice, such as 30 days' notice, before the end of the current billing cycle. This gives you time to find a replacement client and gives the client time to find a new provider.

Specify what happens to prepaid fees if the client cancels early. You might refund the unused portion or apply it to the final visit. Be fair and consistent. If you need to cancel due to illness or equipment failure, state that you will make every effort to reschedule or provide a credit for the missed visit.

A simple, clear agreement builds trust and keeps your business running smoothly. By defining the scope, schedule, weather policies, billing, and termination terms, you protect both your time and your client's expectations. Review your agreement annually to ensure it still fits your business model and local conditions. For specific legal requirements, always check local rules or consult a professional.
